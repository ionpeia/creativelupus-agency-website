Me & Me

A horror short. 15 minutes. London.

One house. One bathroom. One thing that has been living in her for years.

Logline

Delilah heads home after an ordinary day at work. Waiting inside is the most terrifying fight of her life, against an enemy who knows her better than anyone.

The story

A woman in her thirties is alone in her own home, and alone with her addiction to heroin. She has made the house a fortress. The thing she is fighting has found its way in anyway: in the mirrors, in the pipes, in the quiet between rooms.

Over fifteen minutes the house closes in around her, from hallway to landing to bathroom, until the two of them are finally in the same room. She resists. It is patient. It never raises its voice.

The monster within

People have always given appetite a face. In the oldest stories, the demon of comfort never drags anyone anywhere. It sits down beside you, in the smallest room of the house, and offers an easier way.

Addiction works the same way. The monster in Me & Me is not an intruder. It's a lodger: familiar, reasonable, and wearing her face.

The ending

She dies of an overdose, in her own bathroom.

The film doesn't rescue her and it doesn't punish her. We don't show preparation, and there is nothing in the frame to learn from or to want. We stay on the room, and then on the empty house.

A closing card will point viewers to UK support services.

What it's about

Addiction is not a stranger.
It is patient, polite, and familiar.
And it rarely has an audience.

Me & Me looks at the private, domestic side of addiction, the part that happens behind a front door. We tell it as horror because that is how it feels from the inside.

The house

We shoot in London, inside a real Victorian house: high ceilings, period tiles, a long mirror, a deep bath. The architecture does half the work.

One address, a small crew, and a contained shoot. The film moves through the house room by room, and the camera ends where she does, in the bathroom.

The lead

A woman in her thirties. She carries every scene, and she plays both sides of the mirror: herself, and the thing that wears her face.

Casting open
